# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 From Feature Representation to Quantitative Morphology Geometry

The central finding of this study is that quantitative representation of Clo-Sket garment sketches reveals structured morphology geometry rather than an unorganized collection of independent measurements.

The 135-dimensional representation was constructed directly from image geometry and contained both spatial occupancy measurements and global geometric descriptors. After source-only standardization, morphology variance was strongly concentrated in a substantially smaller set of principal directions. This concentration indicates that the measured morphology properties exhibit substantial covariance rather than varying independently across the full feature space.

More importantly, dimensionality reduction was followed by evidence of local neighborhood structure and global graph connectivity. Nearby observations exhibited progressively smaller relative changes as neighborhood size increased, while the 10-nearest-neighbor morphology graph connected all 2,300 sketches within a single component. Successive local transitions also remained relatively close to the characteristic local morphology scale.

Together, these findings indicate that the representation captures quantitative relationships among sketches rather than merely assigning independent numerical descriptors to them. Differences between garment sketches can therefore be examined as structured quantitative variation within a common morphology space.

The distinction between quantitative geometry and semantic interpretation is important. The present results establish measurable relationships among morphology observations, but they do not identify semantic units or named morphology concepts. Likewise, the absence of a globally directed trajectory does not support an ordered sequence of morphology states.

The 73-dimensional PCA representation and the approximately 10.55-dimensional participation-ratio effective dimensionality describe variance concentration and effective representation complexity. They should not be interpreted as establishing an exact mathematical manifold dimension.

The most defensible interpretation is therefore that garment sketches occupy a **structured quantitative morphology space** in which measurable variation is relational, locally organized, and globally connected.

This quantitative geometry provides the basis for examining whether recurring density organization and regional morphology profiles occur within the same space. Whether such quantitative structures correspond to semantic morphology units remains a separate empirical question.


## 5.2 Structured Continuous Variation Rather Than Demonstrated Discrete Categories

The morphology geometry identified in this study is better characterized as **structured quantitative variation containing recurring regional organization** than as a collection of discrete morphology categories.

Several observations support this interpretation. Local morphology transitions were relatively smooth, with successive nearest-neighbor transitions remaining close to the characteristic one-step distance. The morphology graph was also globally connected, with all 2,300 observations belonging to a single connected component.

The multiscale density analysis nevertheless revealed recurring regions of elevated observational density. Importantly, these regions did not behave like conventional compact clusters. Within-region morphology distances were not consistently smaller than between-region distances, while local same-region neighbor retention was substantial. Regional feature profiles also exhibited negative silhouette values, indicating considerable overlap.

This combination of findings is important. The data demonstrate that observations can repeatedly concentrate in particular regions of the quantitative morphology space while retaining substantial variation within and between those regions.

The resulting organization can therefore be described as:

    quantitative morphology space
                ↓
    recurring density organization
                ↓
    overlapping quantitative profiles
                ↓
    continuous quantitative variation

The density regions should therefore be interpreted as **recurring regions of quantitative morphology organization**, rather than as discrete morphology classes.

This interpretation is also consistent with the scale-dependent nature of the density landscapes. Changes in observational scale altered the number and membership of density regions, while quantitative regional profile structure remained reproducible beyond the size-preserving permutation null. The organization therefore exhibits reproducibility across scales without producing a single invariant partition.

For the broader morphology-language hypothesis, this distinction is consequential. The present evidence shows that quantitative morphology is structured, but it does not establish whether that structure corresponds to a vocabulary of discrete morphology concepts.

The recurring regions cannot yet be called morphological "words," "concepts," or "primitives." Establishing such correspondence would require independent semantic or perceptual validation.


## 5.3 Why Recurring Density Organization Matters

The identification of recurring density organization within a structured quantitative morphology space provides an intermediate level of description between individual measurements and any future semantic interpretation.

A purely feature-level description treats each garment sketch as a point defined by a collection of quantitative measurements. The geometry results show that these measurements participate in a connected morphology space in which some quantitative configurations occur more frequently than others.

The multiscale density analysis provides evidence for this recurring organization. Across independently derived observational scales, density landscapes repeatedly produced regions of elevated observational density. Although the number and membership of individual regions changed with scale, quantitative morphology profiles associated with those regions remained reproducible.

This distinction between region identity and profile reproducibility is important. A density region is not treated as a fixed entity that must retain identical membership at every scale. Instead, the result concerns the reproducibility of **quantitative regional profile structure** despite changes in the exact boundaries and membership of the density regions.

Such scale-dependent recurrence is more consistent with structured quantitative morphology variation than with a single categorical partition.

The importance of these regions therefore lies not in their serving as predefined classes, but in revealing where quantitative morphology configurations repeatedly concentrate within the broader morphology space.

The regional structures also showed a measurable relationship to the continuous quantitative geometry. Properties associated with variation through the morphology space overlapped with properties that distinguished the recurring density regions. Thus, the regional organization is related to quantitative variation already present in the morphology representation rather than being treated as an independently imposed semantic partition.

At the same time, the absence of strong global compactness and the negative silhouette values are informative. They indicate that the recurring regions cannot simply be interpreted as well-separated morphology classes. Their significance instead arises from the combination of:

- local same-region coherence,
- reproducibility of quantitative regional profiles,
- scale-dependent recurrence, and
- measurable differences in morphology properties.

The resulting interpretation remains entirely quantitative. Whether these recurring structures correspond to human-perceived morphology concepts is not established by the present analysis.


## 5.4 From Quantitative Morphology Structure Toward Future Semantic Investigation

The results provide a quantitative basis for considering garment sketches as structured visual objects rather than collections of unrelated measurements.

The present study establishes several lower-level properties:

    image morphology
          ↓
    quantitative morphology representation
          ↓
    structured morphology geometry
          ↓
    recurring density organization
          ↓
    reproducible quantitative regional profiles

These results provide a foundation for investigating whether higher-level semantic organization exists, but they do not themselves establish such organization.

Multiple quantitative properties contribute to the morphology geometry, and overlapping properties participate in regional differences. This indicates that morphology variation is distributed across combinations of spatial occupancy and global geometric measurements rather than being reducible to a small number of isolated scalar measurements.

This observation motivates—but does not establish—the possibility that future semantic representations may need to model relationships among multiple morphology properties rather than assigning isolated semantic labels to individual measurements.

The distinction is critical.

The present study does **not** demonstrate that:

    quantitative feature
            =
    semantic primitive

nor that:

    density region
            =
    morphology category

nor that:

    regional relationships
            =
    morphology grammar

Instead, the present study establishes the quantitative substrate on which those questions can subsequently be tested.

The appropriate progression is therefore:

    quantitative morphology geometry
                  ↓
        recurring quantitative
             organization
                  ↓
        independent perceptual
            validation
                  ↓
       semantic interpretation
                  ↓
     possible morphology vocabulary
                  ↓
     possible compositional grammar

Only the first two levels are addressed by the present study.


## 5.5 What the Present Evidence Establishes — and What It Does Not

The findings support a specific and bounded conclusion about the organization of garment-sketch morphology.

At the quantitative level, the evidence demonstrates that Clo-Sket sketches occupy a structured morphology geometry rather than an arbitrary collection of independent feature measurements. This geometry is characterized by concentrated variance, global graph connectivity, locally smooth quantitative transitions, and recurring multiscale density organization.

At the regional level, independently derived density regions exhibit measurable quantitative morphology differences. Their quantitative profiles show reproducibility across observational scales that exceeds a size-preserving permutation null.

At the representation level, morphology geometry is distributed across multiple complementary feature families. Horizontal occupancy, vertical occupancy, and global morphology descriptors each contribute measurable structural information, while whole-block perturbation demonstrates that the geometry is not controlled by a small number of isolated measurements.

The feature-order audit provides an additional, narrower result. Ordered horizontal and vertical occupancy profiles exhibit strong local spatial adjacency relative to a within-observation coordinate-order permutation null. This establishes **spatial locality in the ordered occupancy representation**. It does not establish semantic ordering, morphology primitives, categories, or grammar.

Together, the evidence supports the following empirical statement:

> **Garment sketches exhibit structured quantitative morphology organization within a connected morphology geometry, including recurring density-organized regions with reproducible quantitative morphology profiles.**

Several stronger interpretations remain unsupported.

First, recurring density regions cannot yet be identified as semantic morphology categories. Their global compactness is weak, their profiles overlap, and their boundaries vary with observational scale.

Second, individual quantitative features cannot yet be designated as semantic morphology primitives. Their association with geometric variation establishes measurable morphological relevance, not semantic identity.

Third, no morphology grammar has been demonstrated. The present analysis does not establish compositional rules governing how morphology properties combine to form complete garment structures.

Fourth, feature perturbation demonstrates structural influence within the representation, not causal importance in garment design.

Finally, neither the 73-dimensional PCA representation nor the approximately 10.55-dimensional participation-ratio effective dimensionality should be interpreted as the exact dimension of a mathematical morphology manifold.

These boundaries are central to the interpretation of the study. The present work establishes quantitative morphology organization; it does not establish its semantic meaning.


## 5.6 Limitations and Future Empirical Tests

### 5.6.1 Dataset Scope

The present geometry was established using the Clo-Sket source dataset comprising 2,300 sketches. The extent to which the observed morphology organization generalizes to other sketch collections, drawing styles, garment categories, designers, or annotation protocols remains to be established.

An important next test is external replication using independently collected garment-sketch datasets. Reproduction of the major spectral, geometric, and multiscale regional findings would provide stronger evidence that the observed organization reflects general properties of garment-sketch morphology rather than characteristics specific to Clo-Sket.


### 5.6.2 Representation Dependence

The present findings are conditional on the canonical 135-dimensional morphology representation defined in the methodology.

Although feature-block complementarity and perturbation analyses demonstrate that the observed geometry is distributed across the three feature families, alternative quantitative representations may recover additional or different aspects of morphology structure.

Future work should therefore test whether the major geometric properties identified here remain stable under independently constructed morphology representations.

Such analyses would distinguish structure intrinsic to garment morphology from structure introduced by a particular feature-engineering scheme.


### 5.6.3 Semantic Grounding

The recurring density regions and measurable morphology properties identified in this study are quantitative structures. Their correspondence to human-recognizable morphology concepts has not been established.

A direct next experiment is to obtain independent human judgments about morphology similarity, regional organization, or specific morphological properties and test their correspondence with the quantitative geometry.

Agreement between independently derived quantitative organization and human-perceived morphology organization would provide evidence for semantic grounding.

Conversely, systematic disagreement would identify where the quantitative representation captures geometric structure that is not perceptually or semantically salient.


### 5.6.4 Validation of Semantic Morphology Primitives

The present study does not establish semantic morphology primitives.

A stronger primitive claim would require independent validation that a candidate quantitative property or combination of properties corresponds consistently to a recognizable morphological unit across sketches.

Future experiments should therefore test whether independently selected observers can identify candidate morphology properties reliably and whether such properties remain stable across different garment contexts.

This would provide a direct empirical test of the transition:

    quantitative morphology property
                  ↓
    perceptually meaningful morphology property
                  ↓
    semantic morphology unit

rather than assuming that statistical importance implies semantic status.


### 5.6.5 Morphology Categories and Grammar

The present study does not establish morphology categories or a morphology grammar.

A category claim would require evidence that quantitative regions correspond reliably to independently defined or human-recognized classes.

A grammar claim would require an additional level of evidence demonstrating reproducible compositional relationships among independently validated morphology units.

These are distinct empirical questions from identifying quantitative morphology organization.

Future work should therefore first establish whether candidate quantitative structures have independent perceptual or semantic validity. Only after such validation should compositional relationships be tested.


### 5.6.6 External and Held-Out Validation

The present analyses establish morphology geometry and regional organization within the source dataset. Stronger evidence would be obtained by evaluating frozen transformations and representations on independently held-out sketches.

In particular, future validation should test whether:

    source morphology representation
                  ↓
          frozen transformation
                  ↓
          morphology geometry
                  ↓
      regional quantitative structure

can be reproduced without refitting the underlying transformations to evaluation data.

Such validation would strengthen the claim that the observed organization represents reproducible morphology structure rather than source-specific geometry.


### 5.6.7 Structural Influence versus Causality

Feature perturbation demonstrates that different feature families contribute to preservation of the observed morphology geometry. However, perturbational influence within a representation should not be interpreted as causal influence in garment construction or design.

Establishing causal morphology relationships would require interventions defined at the level of garment structure or controlled generation of morphology changes.

The present findings therefore remain appropriately limited to structural contribution within the quantitative representation.


### 5.6.8 From Quantitative Morphology to Semantic Language

The central unresolved question is whether the structured quantitative morphology space identified here can be grounded in a semantic system.

The present evidence supports:

    quantitative morphology representation
                  ↓
    structured quantitative geometry
                  ↓
    recurring regional organization
                  ↓
    reproducible quantitative morphology profiles

The next empirical stage must test:

    quantitative morphology structure
                  ↓
    independent perceptual validation
                  ↓
    semantic morphology interpretation
                  ↓
    validated morphology units
                  ↓
    possible morphology vocabulary
                  ↓
    possible compositional grammar

This progression should be tested incrementally rather than assuming that geometric organization is itself semantic language.

The present study therefore establishes the quantitative geometric substrate from which such a language can be investigated. It does not claim to have already recovered the semantic language of garment sketches.

The central limitation is consequently also the central opportunity:

> **We have demonstrated structured quantitative morphology space. The next question is whether that structure can be independently grounded as a semantic system.**
