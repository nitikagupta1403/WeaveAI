# 1. Introduction

Garment sketches are compact visual representations of garment form before physical realization. Their silhouettes, proportions, internal lines, and spatial organization convey substantial information about design structure, yet a sketch is not simply a reduced photograph of a garment. It is an abstract geometric description in which aspects of design intent are expressed through shape. This makes garment sketches relevant not only to recognition, retrieval, generation, and garment modelling, but also to a more fundamental computational question: how is garment morphology quantitatively organized across a population of sketches?

Computational research involving fashion sketches has largely been organized around task-specific objectives, including sketch-based garment modelling, recognition and retrieval, style transfer, and sketch-to-fashion generation [CITATIONS]. These studies demonstrate that sketch geometry can support downstream inference and synthesis. However, comparatively less attention has been given to the representation problem that precedes those tasks: whether garment morphology can be encoded using explicit, interpretable measurements; whether such measurements exhibit reproducible population-level organization; and whether alternative geometric coordinate systems capture overlapping or complementary aspects of sketch structure.

This distinction is important because success on a downstream task does not by itself explain which geometric properties of the sketch representation carry useful information. Learned image embeddings may achieve strong predictive performance while obscuring the coordinates through which shape is represented. Conversely, an explicit geometric representation makes it possible to inspect variation, neighborhood structure, cross-representation association, and the incremental value of distinct descriptor families. The present study therefore treats garment-sketch morphology as a quantitative geometric object rather than beginning with a semantic vocabulary or an opaque learned embedding.

We first represent each sketch using a frozen 135-dimensional morphology vector composed of 64 horizontal occupancy measurements, 64 vertical occupancy measurements, and seven global geometric descriptors. The occupancy coordinates retain direct spatial meaning by measuring foreground distribution along the two canonical image axes, while the global descriptors summarize broader geometric properties. Across 2,300 sketches, these vectors define an explicit morphology matrix in which population-level variation and neighborhood organization can be evaluated directly.

We then construct a second representation from the same source sketches using an independently defined radial–angular coordinate system. Radial and angular descriptions provide a natural way to characterize how sketch structure is distributed around an image-derived centroid and across distance from that centroid [CITATIONS]. The resulting 28-dimensional representation contains five predefined blocks: F₂ radial descriptors, α₂ descriptors, observed circular descriptors, learned circular descriptors, and relational descriptors. This compact representation is not proposed as a uniquely optimal or universal description of garment sketches. Instead, it provides an alternative geometric coordinate system whose relationship with explicit morphology can be tested empirically.

The two representations enable three related but distinct questions. First, do individual morphology coordinates exhibit measurable associations with independently derived radial–angular quantities? Second, can radial–angular measurements be recovered from morphology for held-out sketches, indicating that the two coordinate systems share quantitative structure? Third, does radial–angular geometry contribute downstream category-discriminative information beyond morphology when the two representations are combined?

The third question requires particular care because multiple sketches can originate from the same source garment. If sketches belonging to one source identity occur in both training and testing, apparent generalization may partly reflect performance on new renderings of an already represented garment. We therefore distinguish the historical image-level evaluation from a stricter source-identity-grouped design in which complete source-garment identities are withheld from training. The grouped design preserves all garment categories in every fold while preventing any source identity from crossing the training–test boundary. Identity-aware bootstrap resampling and repeated category-balanced grouped partitions are used to assess uncertainty and sensitivity to identity-to-fold allocation.

We additionally examine whether downstream improvement depends specifically on exact held-out sketch-level pairing between morphology and radial–angular descriptors. A within-category alignment control disrupts the pairing between the two representations while preserving garment-category membership and category-level radial–angular distributions. This control distinguishes the general presence of complementary category-discriminative information from the stronger mechanistic claim that downstream gain requires exact sketch-level morphology–radial–angular correspondence.

The study makes three principal contributions. First, it establishes and verifies a frozen, explicit morphology representation for 2,300 garment sketches and evaluates its population-level quantitative organization without using category labels to construct the space. Second, it relates morphology to an independently derived radial–angular representation through multiplicity-controlled feature associations and source-identity-grouped recovery analyses. Third, it evaluates representation-level complementarity under unseen-source-garment classification, supported by identity-aware uncertainty estimates, repeated grouped partitions, radial–angular-only comparison, and a within-category alignment control.

The intended contribution is empirical and representation-focused. We ask whether explicit morphology and radial–angular geometry provide reproducible, partially shared, and potentially complementary descriptions of the tested sketch population. We do not interpret density-defined morphology regions as discrete garment states, and we do not claim semantic garment-part recognition, a universal morphology vocabulary, a garment grammar, causal mechanisms, information-theoretic independence, or human-like visual understanding. Under these boundaries, the study provides an evidence-based framework for analysing garment sketches as quantitatively organized geometric objects and for testing whether alternative explicit representations contribute distinct downstream information.
