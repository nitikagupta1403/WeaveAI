# 1. Introduction

Fashion sketches provide a compact visual representation of garment design in which a relatively small number of lines and geometric marks encode structural information about a garment. Unlike photographs or rendered garments, sketches often abstract away material appearance and other visual detail while retaining geometric cues about changes in width, silhouette, and component organization. This makes fashion sketches a useful setting for studying how visual structure can be represented computationally.

A central challenge is that the structural organization of sketches is difficult to represent without imposing a predefined vocabulary. Existing computational approaches commonly describe sketches using geometric descriptors, strokes, points, semantic components, or topological relationships. Such representations are useful for specific recognition or retrieval tasks, but they do not necessarily provide a corpus-derived vocabulary of recurring geometric structures from which complete sketches can be represented compositionally. In particular, it remains unclear whether recurring geometric units can be learned directly from sketch geometry and whether those units exhibit systematic organization beyond their local geometric form.

This study investigates whether fashion sketches contain recurring geometric structures that can be learned directly from the observed geometry and organized into a reusable symbolic representation. Rather than beginning with predefined garment components or semantic categories, we adopt a geometry-first approach. Continuous sketch geometry is transformed into a one-dimensional width signal, from which localized persistent geometric events are extracted. These events are then represented by normalized local geometric curves and clustered to obtain a learned vocabulary of recurring geometry primitives.

The resulting primitive vocabulary provides a discrete representation of otherwise continuous sketch geometry. We examine whether curves assigned to the same learned primitive exhibit greater morphological coherence than curves assigned to different primitives, thereby testing whether the learned units correspond to recurring geometric patterns rather than arbitrary partitions of the event space.

We next investigate whether the learned primitives exhibit sequential organization within complete garment sketches. Garments are represented as ordered primitive sequences, allowing primitive-to-primitive transitions to be quantified across the corpus. Statistical permutation tests are used to determine whether observed transitions are more or less frequent than expected under randomized within-garment ordering. We further test whether sequential context provides predictive information about subsequent primitives and whether the resulting organization generalizes to garments excluded from estimation.

The representation is then examined at progressively higher structural levels. Primitive identities are characterized by their positional distributions and local sequential neighborhoods, providing measurable structural profiles for individual primitives. These properties are integrated into a primitive-level knowledge representation. We additionally examine whether a predefined higher-level family taxonomy corresponds to the structural organization learned from the geometry, rather than assuming such correspondence a priori.

Finally, complete garments are represented as ordered compositions of the learned primitives. This allows the study to examine whether a relatively small shared vocabulary can describe the structural organization of complete garment sketches while individual garments instantiate different subsets of that vocabulary.

Together, these analyses address a broader question: **can recurring geometric organization in fashion sketches be learned from visual evidence and represented as a structured system of reusable components and their relationships?**

The resulting framework provides a geometry-derived intermediate representation spanning persistent geometric events, learned primitive identity, morphology, sequential organization, structural roles, and compositional garment representations. The sequential regularities identified within this representation provide evidence for a corpus-derived, grammar-like organization of garment geometry.

Importantly, the term *grammar* is used here in a computational and statistical sense. The study does not assume that the learned primitives correspond to human-defined garment parts, nor does it claim to recover a complete linguistic or cognitive grammar of fashion design. Similarly, higher-order semantic interpretations are treated as computational interpretations of relationships established within the learned geometric representation rather than as independently validated human semantic ground truth.

The contribution of this work is therefore a geometry-first computational representation of fashion-sketch structure in which recurring geometric units are learned from the corpus and subsequently examined through their morphology, sequential relationships, structural profiles, and compositional organization.
