# 4. Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that the geometric structure of fashion sketches can be represented through a hierarchy of increasingly expressive computational representations. Let \(\mathcal{V}\) denote the learned Geometric Primitive Vocabulary, \(S\) the Garment Sentence, \(\mathcal{R}\) the Learned Sequential Organization, \(\mathcal{G}\) the Visual Grammar, and \(\mathcal{M}\) the Primitive Morphology. These representations do not constitute independent descriptions of the sketch; rather, each is derived from and retains correspondence with the preceding representation. The resulting hierarchy therefore provides a formal basis for describing how local geometric observations can be progressively organized into reusable components, sequential regularities, morphological variation, and higher-order relational structure.

The sequential representation can be interpreted as a conditional probability structure over the learned primitive vocabulary. For primitive identities \(v_i,v_j \in \mathcal{V}\), the transition probability \(P(v_j \mid v_i)\) describes the empirical tendency of primitive \(v_j\) to follow primitive \(v_i\) within a garment sentence. The resulting transition matrix therefore defines a directed weighted graph over \(\mathcal{V}\), in which edge weights encode corpus-level sequential regularities rather than manually specified design rules. Non-uniform transition probabilities indicate that the occurrence of a primitive is not independent of its sequential context, providing evidence for structured organization at the level of primitive composition. In this formulation, the visual grammar is consequently a statistical representation of observed sequence structure: it captures regularities in how geometry-derived primitives are organized across the corpus without assuming that these regularities constitute explicit design rules or deterministic constraints.

The morphological representation provides a complementary description of primitive identity by distinguishing canonical geometric structure from the variability of its observed realizations. For a geometry primitive \(P_k\), the Primitive Prototype \(\Pi_k\) provides a characteristic geometric representation, while the corresponding Primitive Morphology \(\mathcal{M}\) describes the structured variation of observations relative to that prototype. This distinction is important because symbolic identity and geometric realization need not be equivalent: multiple observations can belong to the same primitive while exhibiting continuous geometric differences. The representation therefore separates discrete structural identity from continuous morphological variation, allowing the learned vocabulary to preserve both recurrence at the symbolic level and variability at the geometric level.

The contextual morphology analysis further suggests that primitive identity alone does not fully determine geometric realization. The Context-Dependent Primitive Morphology \(M_k^{(\Gamma)}\) extends the context-independent morphology by conditioning the geometric realization of primitive \(P_k\) on its local grammatical context \(\Gamma_k\). Differences between context-conditioned and context-independent realizations therefore provide a representation of how the geometry associated with a primitive varies across structural environments. This provides evidence for an interaction between symbolic organization and continuous form: primitive morphology is not necessarily invariant across the grammar in which the primitive occurs. However, the observed association should be interpreted as context-dependent variation rather than causal grammatical deformation, since the present analysis does not establish that sequential context directly produces the observed geometric changes.

The combination of sequential and morphological representations provides a joint description of both structural organization and geometric realization. The sequential representation specifies relationships among primitive identities, whereas the morphological representation specifies relationships among their geometric forms. Their integration therefore defines a representation in which a primitive can be characterized simultaneously by its position within a sequence, its relationships to neighboring primitives, and the geometric form associated with its occurrence. This joint formulation is important because sequential regularity alone does not specify how a primitive is visually realized, while morphological similarity alone does not specify how geometric components are organized within a garment. The resulting representation consequently links symbolic composition with continuous geometric form without reducing either dimension to the other.

Its elements combine information derived from primitive identity, positional organization, grammatical relationships, morphological similarity, and context-dependent geometric variation. Let \(\Sigma\) denote the Semantic Representation defined by the integration of these complementary representations. Its elements combine information derived from primitive identity, positional organization, grammatical relationships, morphological similarity, and relational network structure. In this formulation, semantic organization emerges through the integration of previously characterized computational properties rather than through predefined semantic labels imposed on the geometry. The resulting representation provides a basis for interpreting relationships among primitives at a higher level while preserving traceability to the geometric and structural evidence from which those relationships were derived.


The Semantic Representation can be further organized as an explicit relational structure in which learned semantic entities and their relationships are represented within a common computational graph. Let \(\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{V}_K,\mathcal{E}_K)\) denote the resulting Knowledge Graph, where \(\mathcal{V}_K\) contains learned semantic entities and \(\mathcal{E}_K\) represents the relationships connecting them. This formulation makes the semantic relationships emerging from the integrated representation explicit and provides a common structure in which heterogeneous evidence can be explored jointly. The Knowledge Graph therefore functions as an organizational representation of the semantic structure derived from the preceding hierarchy rather than as an independently curated ontology or a direct representation of human design cognition.

The reasoning layer extends the relational representation by deriving higher-order semantic knowledge from the interconnected structure encoded by \(\mathcal{K}\). Let \(\Omega\) denote the inferred semantic knowledge obtained through the semantic reasoning operator applied to the learned Knowledge Graph. The resulting reasoning process can combine and traverse multiple relationships within the graph, providing a traceable basis for examining higher-order properties of the learned semantic organization. This demonstrates that the proposed representation supports multi-source relational inference rather than relying on a single geometric or sequential attribute. However, the resulting inferences remain conclusions drawn from the constructed computational representation; they should not be interpreted as independently validated semantic understanding or as evidence of human-equivalent design knowledge.

The direction of construction distinguishes this framework from conventional approaches to structured visual understanding. Existing fashion representations such as predefined apparel ontologies generally begin with semantic categories, parts, or attributes and subsequently locate or recognize those categories in visual data. In contrast, the present framework begins with recurring geometric events extracted from fashion sketches and derives a symbolic vocabulary from their observed shape structure before introducing semantic interpretation. The distinction is therefore not simply between different feature sets, but between two directions of representation: from predefined semantics to visual evidence, and from visual evidence to progressively structured representation. The latter provides a corpus-grounded basis for investigating whether semantic organization can emerge from measurable relationships within the sketch itself.

The combined evidence supports interpreting the learned representation as a computationally learnable structured visual grammar. This interpretation does not depend on any single property of the primitive vocabulary; rather, it follows from the coexistence of recurrent primitive identities, non-independent sequential organization, structured morphological variation, and context-associated changes in geometric realization. Formally, the representation can be viewed as a hierarchy in which primitive identity provides the basic state space, sequential relationships define transition structure, and morphological representations describe the continuous realization of those states. Higher-order relational and semantic representations then integrate information derived from these lower levels. The grammar interpretation therefore refers to the organization of measurable relationships within the learned representation, rather than to a predefined symbolic system or an assumed correspondence with human linguistic grammar.

The interpretation of the representation as a structured visual grammar is nevertheless bounded by the design of the present study. The primitive vocabulary is learned under a specified clustering configuration, and therefore its cardinality and composition should not be interpreted as uniquely determined properties of fashion sketches. Similarly, the semantic roles and higher-order organizations are derived from computational measurements and methodological choices rather than from independently validated human semantic annotations. The contextual morphology analysis establishes context-associated variation but does not establish a causal mechanism, while the integrated sequential and morphological representations are corpus-derived and have not been evaluated through an independent held-out test of predictive generalization. These limitations define the scope of the inference: the study demonstrates structured organization within the analyzed corpus and its computational representation, but does not establish universality across designers, datasets, or cultures, nor equivalence between the learned representation and human design cognition.

Taken together, the findings support the central hypothesis that fashion sketches contain recurring visual primitives whose positional, sequential, morphological, and relational organization can be computationally represented as a structured visual grammar. The principal contribution is not a claim of universal semantic language, but a measurable representational pathway from continuous sketch geometry to reusable primitives, structured sequence, contextual morphology, and higher-order relational organization. By constructing this pathway from sketch-derived evidence before introducing semantic interpretation, the framework provides an intermediate computational representation between raw visual geometry and higher-level semantic reasoning. Within the limits of the present corpus and analyses, the results therefore establish a corpus-grounded account of structured visual organization in fashion sketches and define a foundation for future investigation of its generalization, semantic validity, and utility in computational fashion design.
