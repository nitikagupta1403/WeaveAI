# 4. Discussion

The findings indicate that the geometric structure of fashion sketches can be represented at progressively higher levels of abstraction while retaining correspondence with the underlying sketch geometry. Let \(\mathcal{V}\) denote the learned Geometric Primitive Vocabulary, \(S\) the Garment Sentence, \(\mathcal{R}\) the Learned Sequential Organization, \(\mathcal{G}\) the Visual Grammar, and \(\mathcal{M}\) the Primitive Morphology. Each representation is derived from the preceding level and introduces additional information about the organization or realization of the observed geometry. The resulting hierarchy provides a formal basis for relating local geometric observations to reusable components, sequential organization, morphological variation, and higher-order structure.

The learned sequential organization can be interpreted as a first-order probabilistic structure over the primitive vocabulary. For \(v_i,v_j \in \mathcal{V}\), the conditional transition probability \(P(v_j\mid v_i)\) quantifies the corpus-derived tendency for one primitive to follow another. Under this formulation, the Visual Grammar \(\mathcal{G}\) represents statistical regularities in primitive composition, with transition structure providing a measurable description of how local components are organized within garment sequences. These regularities describe the observed corpus rather than prescribing explicit design rules or deterministic constraints.

The morphological representation adds a continuous dimension to the discrete primitive vocabulary. For a primitive \(P_k\), the prototype \(\Pi_k\) represents its characteristic geometric form, while the associated morphology represents deviations of observed instances from that prototype. Primitive identity can therefore remain discrete while its geometric realization varies continuously. This distinction allows the representation to separate structural recurrence from geometric variability, preserving information that would be lost if each primitive were treated as a fixed shape.

The geometric form of a primitive can vary depending on the structural context in which it occurs. For primitive \(P_k\), the context-dependent representation \(M_k^{(\Gamma)}\) describes its geometric realization under a specified grammatical context \(\Gamma_k\). Differences between context-independent and context-conditioned realizations therefore indicate that a primitive does not necessarily have a single invariant geometric form across all structural environments. This links sequential organization to continuous geometric variation while remaining an associative result: the observed contextual differences do not establish that grammatical context causally produces the corresponding geometric changes.


Sequential organization and morphology therefore describe different properties of the same primitive system. The former characterizes relationships among primitive identities, whereas the latter characterizes variation in their geometric realization. Their combination provides a representation in which structural position and sequential context can be considered together with the continuous form of the corresponding primitive. This integration is important because a sequence of primitive identities does not fully specify their geometric realization, while geometric similarity alone does not capture how components are organized within a garment.

At the semantic level, the learned relationships are assigned higher-order interpretations that describe the organization and role of primitives within the representation. The semantic representation \(\Sigma\) therefore provides an interpretive layer over the established computational structure rather than introducing a separate source of evidence. This distinction keeps semantic interpretation traceable to the measured relationships while recognizing that the interpretation itself is a higher-order construction.


The Knowledge Graph \(\mathcal{K}\) makes the learned relationships explicit by representing semantic entities as nodes and their relationships as edges. It provides a common structure for integrating and examining these relationships without implying that the graph constitutes a fixed semantic ontology or a model of human design cognition.

The reasoning layer extends this representation by deriving higher-order information from the relationships encoded in \(\mathcal{K}\). The resulting semantic knowledge \(\Omega\) therefore represents inference over the learned relational structure rather than an independently observed semantic ground truth.

The significance of the framework lies in linking these representations through a common geometry-derived vocabulary. The individual computational procedures are established methods; their combination provides a unified representation in which geometric identity, sequential organization, morphological variation, and semantic relationships can be examined within the same system. The resulting contribution is therefore primarily representational, providing a computational account of sketch structure that remains traceable from higher-order representations to the underlying geometry.

The direction of construction constitutes a central distinction of the framework. Rather than beginning with predefined semantic categories and subsequently locating those categories within visual data, the present approach begins with measurable geometric structure and progressively constructs symbolic, sequential, morphological, and relational representations before semantic interpretation is introduced. In this sense, the framework reverses the direction from predefined semantics to visual evidence: semantic organization is investigated as a higher-order consequence of relationships discovered within the visual representation itself. The resulting formulation provides a corpus-grounded pathway from continuous sketch geometry to structured symbolic representation and, ultimately, to interpretable semantic organization.

The joint representation exhibits the properties expected of a corpus-derived visual grammar: a discrete vocabulary of recurring primitives, probabilistic sequential relationships among those primitives, and systematic variation in their geometric realization across structural contexts. These properties define \(\mathcal{G}\) as a statistical representation of component organization, in which primitive identities provide the representational units, transition probabilities characterize their sequential relationships, and morphological representations describe their geometric realization. The grammar is therefore an empirical model of organization observed in the corpus, rather than a predefined rule system or a claim about human linguistic grammar.


The grammar described here is corpus-derived and representation-dependent. Its structure reflects the particular visual regularities captured by the learned vocabulary and the subsequent sequential and morphological representations. The resulting organization should therefore be understood as a measurable property of the learned representation rather than as a uniquely determined grammar of fashion design. Establishing whether the same structure persists across independent datasets, designers, or sketching conventions would require additional validation.


The semantic layer requires a separate evidential distinction. The representation supports higher-order semantic organization by integrating relationships established at lower levels, but the resulting semantic roles are not independently validated against human annotations or an external semantic ground truth. They should therefore be treated as computational interpretations of the learned structure rather than as direct measurements of semantic meaning. This distinction preserves the interpretability of the semantic representation while limiting the claim to what is supported by the present evidence.


The scientific value of the framework lies in establishing an intermediate representation between sketch geometry and higher-order semantic analysis. This representation makes the structural organization of fashion sketches explicit and provides a basis for examining how geometric regularities support increasingly abstract computational representations.

Taken together, the findings indicate that recurring geometric structure in fashion sketches can be organized into a computational representation spanning primitive identity, sequential organization, morphological variation, and higher-order semantic organization. The evidence therefore supports the formulation of fashion sketches as structured visual systems with a computationally learnable grammar, while leaving the generality and human semantic validity of that grammar open to further investigation.
