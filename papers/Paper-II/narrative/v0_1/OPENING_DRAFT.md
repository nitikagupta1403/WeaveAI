# Auditing and Allocating Representation Complexity in Radial–Spectral Garment Morphology

Narrative revision draft. The title remains provisional. This is an opening draft, not a complete replacement manuscript.

## Abstract

A garment sketch can be described by how its ink is arranged around a centre and how that arrangement changes with distance. This produces a structured representation in which angular harmonics describe different scales of angular variation. We ask what this representation measures and how much radial detail it needs. Using 2,300 sketches of 230 garment identities across 23 categories, we enlarged the surrounding canvas while keeping the garment pixels unchanged. The historical canvas-relative coordinates produced systematic changes in spectral allocation; a matched object-relative construction remained numerically invariant. In a separate selection study frozen before this audit, we tested radial compression within four harmonic bands using garment-identity-disjoint validation. The supported allocation combined compact encodings in the lowest and highest bands with full radial detail in the intermediate bands, reducing the coefficient count by 41.98%. A uniform representation with a similar coefficient budget achieved closely comparable descriptive retrieval performance. Tested nonlinear latent models did not establish an advantage over same-dimensional PCA, whose directions could be mapped back to the retained radial–harmonic field. These results show why a compact sketch representation should be evaluated together with its coordinate system: the surrounding frame can affect the measured structure, and compression choices remain conditional on that measurement.

## 1. Introduction

Consider the garment sketch in Figure 1. Before compressing it into a short vector, we need a description of its structure. One way to build that description is to place a centre in the drawing and ask two simple questions: how far away is the ink, and in which direction does it lie?

These questions give the sketch a radial and an angular coordinate. At each radial distance, we collect the ink around a shell and describe its distribution over angle. A Fourier transform then separates that angular signal into harmonics. Lower harmonics describe angular variation with fewer oscillations; higher harmonics describe variation with more oscillations. Keeping their values across radial shells gives a field indexed by distance and harmonic order. Figure 1 develops this construction step by step using one real sketch.

This field makes the representation's coordinates explicit, but it also creates two decisions. First, what sets the radial scale? Second, how much information should be kept along the radial direction? A third decision follows when the field is summarized by a smaller set of latent coordinates: can we trace those coordinates back to the represented structure?

The first decision is easy to overlook. In the historical representation studied here, distance was normalized by the maximum radius of the entire raster grid around the ink centroid. Enlarging the canvas can therefore move unchanged ink into different normalized radial shells. Because the representation describes the angular distribution within each shell, a change in shell membership can also change its harmonic content. Blank surrounding space may thus affect the measured description even though the garment pixels remain identical.

We test this dependence directly. The same garment patch is placed inside progressively larger canvases, without resizing or resampling its pixels. We compare the historical canvas-relative construction with a matched construction whose radial scale is defined by the foreground. The audit covers 2,300 sketches, grouped into 230 garment identities and 23 categories. The example explains the mechanism; the full dataset establishes the reported pattern.

The second decision concerns compression. Each harmonic has a sequence of values across radial shells. We could retain every value, or apply the same compact encoding to all harmonics. Instead, the historical selection study tested radial encodings separately within four prespecified harmonic bands. Training data selected candidate encodings, and held-out garment identities tested the resulting choices. The supported allocation retained compact encodings at the lowest and highest tested bands and full radial detail in the intermediate bands. It reduced the number of coefficients by 41.98%, although a uniform representation with a similar budget remained competitive in descriptive retrieval.

These two studies have a specific chronology: the compression selection was frozen first, and the support audit was conducted later. We explain the coordinate system before compression because that order helps the reader understand what is being compressed. We do not treat the later audit as a redesign or revalidation of the historical selection.

Finally, we examine a compact latent summary of the selected representation. The tested autoencoder and variational-autoencoder comparisons did not establish an advantage over same-dimensional PCA. PCA also provides an inverse path from a latent direction to changes in the retained radial–harmonic field. That path identifies where a direction acts in the representation; it does not assign garment-part meanings or reconstruct information discarded during compression.

The study therefore connects three concrete decisions: choosing the measurement coordinates, allocating radial detail, and tracing a compact summary back to those coordinates. Polar and Fourier descriptions are established tools. The contribution here is the controlled support audit and the evidence-based evaluation of these decisions within a single garment-sketch representation. The results remain specific to this dataset, coordinate construction, and set of tested encodings.
